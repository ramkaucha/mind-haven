### Risk-aversion
Not willing to take risk unless there is an adequate reward for doing so

Risk-averse - A is preferred to B
Risk-neutral - No preference between A and B
Risk-seeking - B is preferred to A

Risk-averse people could prefer either A or C to B
![[Pasted image 20250118202239.png]]
A is preferred to B - Lower risk for same expected value
C is preferred to B - Higher expected value for same risk
No clear preference for A or C - Lower risk-aversion may prefer C, higher risk-aversion may prefer A

The amount of risk-neutral person would pay to eliminate risk

### The logic of insurance
A person who is risk-averse (rational), should remove pure risk (downside risk) by paying a regular premium as long as that premium is 'reasonable'

Asymmetric information 
	The person being insured and the insurance company have different information 
	The person being insured does not want to pass on negative information about themselves 
	This can make it hard for the insurance company to determine an appropriate premium 
Adverse selection 
	All the ‘low-risk’ people are not willing to pay the insurance premium
	Only the ‘high-risk’ people are willing to pay the insurance premium 
	Providing insurance in this case may not be possible (market failure) 
Moral hazard
	Someone who has taken out insurance transfers risk to the insurance company 
	They may have little or no incentive to mitigate or avoid the risk (bad behaviour) 
	Insurance provider may need to monitor or provide incentives to encourage good behaviour

### MEAT the risk-management alternatives
1. Mitigate the risk - take steps to reduce the probability and/or impact of the bad outcome
2. Eliminate the risk - Remove yourself from the risky situation completely
3. Accept the risk - Set aside some funds (financial slack) to effectively 'self-insure'
4. Transfer the risk, by paying the premium - Buy insurance, transfer or share the risk with a third-party

### Risk management steps
1. Identify risks
	What can go wrong with each component of your financial and investment strategy
	What can go wrong with your career strategy or health
2. Visualise and document the risk
	Visualise in detail how the risk could 'play out' and document it
	Are there links between different risks that could cause a cascade
3. Evaluate the risk
	What is the likelihood (probability) and impact (severity) of each risk?
4. Identify how to MEAT the challenges of each risk
	Mitigate, eliminate, accept or transfer the risk
5. Take action now and then monitor

Many people do not manage risks well

They don't spend time identifying and planning for risks
They do not maintain a cash buffer or financial slack
They fail to take steps to look after physical and mental health
They fail to continuously train to keep employment skills up-to-date
Thy are too inflexible after losing a job
They maintain too high operating leverage (too many fixed costs)
They don't adequately diversify 
They don't get ahead on their home loan payments
Too financially leverage (too much debt and interest expenses)

## Risk Pooling

Scenario:
There are 10 houses in a street They all worry about being burgled … but let us assume that there is no insurance available Each house has the same chance of being burgled The expected amount stolen in a burglary is $10,000 On average, 1 house is burgled per year 10% probability of being burgled each year … with expected cost of 0.1 x 10,000 = $1,000 per year

1. Mitigate - add security systems, barred windows, remove automatic garage doors
2. Eliminate - add fences, and wires, high-security doors, dogs, security guards
3. Accept - save some financial slack to allow for stolen (self-insurance)
4. Transfer - transfer or share the risk with others via risk-pooling
	Every household puts $1,000 into a central account each year
	They are required to take 'reasonable' actions to mitigate risk
	Any house burgled in any given year can claim from funds in the 'pool'
		If there are no burglaries, then the pool is 'rolled over' into the following year
		if there is not enough money to claims then bad luck

It can be better to transfer risk when
1. impact of the event is high
2. probability of event occurring is low
3. premiums are reasonable given the impact and probability
	a 'premium' is the monthly payment you pay to the insurance company

Better to accept risk when its the opposite

Shared pain via a deductible
Insurance companies want you to behave in a 'low-risk' way
they make sure it 'costs you' if the event happens
You must pay a 'deductible' or 'excess' if the insured event occurs
e.g. you pay first $500 of expenses if motor accident is your fault, you pay first $1,000 of medical expenses for an elective surgery

### Screening
Insurance companies don't want 'bad apples' in a risk-pool
Each person completes detailed applications forms
If you are 'high probability' of experiencing 'event' they
1. Won't provide you with insurance or
2. add an 'exclusion' clause to your policy or
3. Charge a ridiculously high premium

### Signalling
Applicants need to 'signal' they are low-risk and honest
Complete the detailed application forms accurately
If dishonest;
1. they may still collect your premiums
2. your claim is rejected and no payout occurs
3. you go onto 'black list' and can't get insurance

Insurance is not a 'discount club'.
Risk pooling doesn't work this way:
1. you are high risk so excluded from risk pool
2. How can a risk-pool work if everyone does this?
3. Insurance is not just about 'the dollars' its about peace of mind
