Represents part ownership of a company
With personal finances, `Wealth = Equity = Assets - Liabilities (Debt)`
With company `Shareholder Wealth = Equity = Assests - Liabilities (Debt)`
Shares divide ownership of equity to different people
Shares are also called *Stocks or Equities*

**Dividends**
When a company makes a profit:
1. Part is retained by company to fund future projects
2. Part is paid to shareholders as cash
Profits paid out to shareholders = *dividends*
Usually paid every 6 months
Paid directly into your nominated bank account
Dividend yield = Dividend per year / Share price
Usually between 0% and 5% per year

**What do shares entitle you**
1% of company's share means
	1% of all future profits paid as dividends, votes for who is on board of directors; assets less debt if the company is bankrupt; votes on 'special actions', the right to participate in share buy-backs, 1% of value of shares if company is taken over

# Valuing shares
total price (7-10%) = Price gains (4-6%) + Dividend yield (3-4%)
Dividends come from the company's profits
	profit = revenue (income) - expenses - tax
	average is 3-4% (but varies between countries due to tax)

The price gains come from
All future profits paid as dividends – YES
Votes for who is on board of directors – NO 
Assets less debt if the company is bankrupt – NO 
Votes on ‘special actions’ – NO 
The right to participate in share buy-backs – YES, BUT SMALL 
Value of shares if company is taken over – YES, BUT UNLIKELY

Dividends are expect to grow with profits

## Valuing growing dividends as a perpetuity
$P_{\infty, g} = \frac{C_1}{r - g}$ , $g <r$
$C_1$ is the next dividend growing at a rate of 'g' per period
$\infty$ means that they go on forever (with first cash flow at end of first period)
$r$ is the required rate of return per period (time value of money)
$P$ is value of the infinite series of cash flows of 'C' at the start

**Examples**
How much should Susan pay for a share that will pay a dividend of $1 in one year growing at a real rate of 2.5% p.a. forever if the required (or expected) real rate of return on that share is 6.5% per annum?
$P_{\infty, g} = \frac{C_1}{r - g}$

$=\frac{1}{0.065 - 0.025}$
$=25.00$

The share price is also expected to grow by 'g', if dividends grows by g% in perpetuity then so will the stock price

**What about shares that don't pay dividends**
The share is still worth something (and possible a lot)
1. Expected to pay big dividends in the future or,
2. Expected to be bought out by some other company

## What drives the returns on a particular shares
According to the Capital Asset Pricing Model
1. Interest rates - expected returns on shares must be higher than interest rates
2. The returns on an average share (Market index) - inflation and productivity growth influenced by the domestic and world economy
3. Sensitivity of the share to the economy - systematic risk (beta)
4. information specific to that company or industry

1. There must be sound reason why share prices go up
2. Future dividends are most important than most people think
3. Price growth comes from unexpected future profit and dividend growth
4. This is also what makes shares risky in the short term future growth is uncertain
5. For the average share, over the long-term, price gains should be linked to inflation and productivity growth
6. Total returns includes these price gains plus dividend yield

# Investment Risk

## Key types of investment risk
Default risk
	chance of losing most or all of your investment (catastrophic loss)
	can be significantly reduced through diversification
Total risk (sigma $\sigma$)
	uncertainty or volatility of future returns (standard deviation)
	can be reduced through diversification
Systematic risk (beta $\beta$)
	Sensitivity of investment returns to economic risks
	Difficult to reduce through diversification

## What drives investment returns in the long-run?
It makes sense to reduce total risk for a target expected return according to the logic of Capital Asset Pricing Model
1. Higher total risk does not necessarily increase expected returns
2. Higher systematic risk does increase expected returns
3. Higher systematic risk often involves higher total risk (not always)
4. A better economy does increase expected returns
5. Higher interest rates do increase expected returns
so the more sensitive the returns for a particular investment to movements in the economy and the better the expected performance of the economy the higher the expected return over the long run

## Important implications
1. It makes sense to diversity to reduce risk if there is no significant difference to expected long-term returns
2. Higher expected long-term returns usually does require selecting investments with higher total and systematic risk
3. The economy has a major impact on long-term returns
4. The sensitivity of an investment's returns to the economy has a major impact on long-term returns
5. Total risk might not matter in the long-term if the returns in one year are unrelated to the returns in the next year

# Portfolio theory

## Expected return of a portfolio of two shares
if we combine the shares of two companies into a portfolio then the expected returns on the portfolio is the weighted average of the two rages
$r_p = w_1 \times r_1 + w_2 \times r_2$
where
$r_p$ is the expected return on a portfolio of shares in two companies
$r_1$ expected return of the first share
$r_2$ expected return on the second share
$w_1$ proportion of the portfolio invested in the first share
$w_2$ proportion of the portfolio invested in the second share
$w_1 + w_2 = 1$

**Example**
What is the expected return on a portfolio of two shares if the expected return of the first one is 7% and the second one is 12%. 40% of the portfolio is invested in the first share and 60% in the second.
$r_p = w_1 \times r_1 + w_2 \times r_2$
$= 7 \times 0.4 + 12 \times 0.6$
$=10$ %

## Total risk of a portfolio of two shares
If we combine the shares of two companies into a portfolio then the total risk ($\sigma$ sigma) of the returns is always less than the weighted average of the standard deviation of each share
The formula for the combined total risk is as follows:
$\sigma_p = \sqrt{w_1^2 \times \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \times \sigma_2^2 \times w_1 \times w_2 \times \rho \times \sigma_1 \times \sigma_2}$
where
$\sigma_p$ standard deviation (total risk) on a portfolio of shares in two companies
$\sigma_1$ standard deviation (total risk) of returns of the first share
$\sigma_2$ standard deviation (total risk) of returns of the second share
$w_1$ proportion of the portfolio invested in the first share
$w_2$ proportion of the portfolio invested in the second share
$\rho$ (rho) correlation in returns between the two shares


**Example**
What is the standard deviation on a portfolio of two shares if the standard deviation of the first one is 20% and the second one is 30%. The correlation ($\rho$) between the two shares is 0.5. 40% of the portfolio is invested in the first share and 60% in the second.
$\sigma_p = \sqrt{w_1^2 \times \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \times \sigma_2^2 \times w_1 \times w_2 \times \rho \times \sigma_1 \times \sigma_2}$
$\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.4^2 \times 20^2 + 0.6^2 \times 30^2 \times 0.4 \times 0.6 \times 0.5 \times 20 \times 30}$
$=23.1$%
The weighted average of standard deviation is 26%
$0.4 \times 20 + 0.6 \times 30 = 26$%

## Benefit of diversification
*Expected returns* - on a portfolio of shares is always equal to the weighted average of the shares
*Total risk* - the standard deviation on a portfolio of shares is always less than the weighted average of the shares

Diversification is powerful way to reduce total risk without necessarily reducing expected returns

1. Both expected return and risk need to be considered
2. Diversification reduces total risk without necessarily reducing expected returns
3. A Broad Market Index often provides a pretty good trade-off between risk and return

# Investing in shares

## Buying shares directly
Buy and sell through broker (usually online)
Fees usually $10 - $20 per transaction
Must research companies thoroughly
Must consider both expected return and risk
Must diversify to reduce risk (usually 10 to 20)
Most investors underperform in either return or risk
Many investors slide into short-term speculative behaviour

## Managed funds (mutual funds)
Offered by banks and investment banks (fund managers)
A professionally designed portfolio of shares or fixed interest promoted through networks of financial advisers
Attempt to 'pick stocks' and 'time the market'
Management fees (usually 1 - 2% per year)
Entry fee (0 - 4% of investment)
Usually pay ongoing commissions to financial advisers
A large choice of funds available (1,000s)
Consistent outperformance in return and risk is debatable

## Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs)
Stock exchange that track an index
Can achieve good diversification with just one share
Low fees compared to mutual funds (managed funds)
Pay no commissions to advisers
Available for many different indices
Less temptation to speculate (timing the market)

## Shares - good and bad

| The Good                                                                                     | The Bad                                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Higher expected returns over the long run                                                    | High uncertainty in returns (total risk) in short and medium term |
| Easy to diversify using ETFs                                                                 | Returns may be low if the economy suffers a long recession        |
| Low transaction fees                                                                         | Tempting to speculate by picking stocks and timing the market     |
| Divisible                                                                                    | More difficult than property to obtain a loan to invest           |
| Low change of losing original investment if you are diversified and invest for the long-term |                                                                   |
| Good for long-term savings (5+ years)                                                        |                                                                   |

## Dividend imputation
Companies pay 30% corporate tax on profits
It then pays after-tax profits to shareholders as dividends
Individuals then pay personal income tax on dividends
This is double-taxation and can lead to 60%+ tax rate
So government refunds any corporate tax to shareholders on dividends paid to them
This is called *dividend imputation*

## Dividends at tax time
You receive a cash dividend of $700
Dividend notice advises 'franking credits' are $300
You report your income as $700 + $300 = $1000
	Even though you only received $700 in cash
Calculate tax at your marginal rate (say 38.5%) = $385
Report the 'franking credit' as a tax rebate on tax return
You receive a tax rebate of $300
You only an extra $85 in tax
Total tax received by government in $300 in corporate tax plus $85 in personal income tax = $385 = 38.5% tax

## Capital gains at tax time
Cost price = Purchase price + brokerage fees
Disposal price = Sale price - brokerage fees
Gain = Disposal price - cost price
Add this gain to your assessable income
Taxed at your marginal rate of tax + medicare levy
If held shares > 1 year then only $\frac{1}{2}$ of gain assessable
You only pay it when you sell the shares so invest for long-term and defer tax indefinitely

# Non-mainstream investments

## Avoid
**Crypto-currencies** - most have little or no 'value in use' since they don't work well as a medium of exchange, they are not a 'financial asset' because they do not generate cash flows
**Derivatives** - 'Created' financial instruments, i.e. You don't really own anything physical, their values are 'derived' from other 'real' assets, such as shares or commodities (gold, oil), very exotic ones are based on anything with a number
**Futures** - Agree to buy / sell an asset at a future time for a set price, make money as prices rise by buying futures, make money as prices fall by selling futures, requires no upfront payment, 'marked-to-market' daily and as prices move, you receive money or pay money from your account
**Contracts for Difference** - Offered by stockbrokers and derivative traders, are similar to futures but there is no fixed term, allow you to make a profit/loss from fluctuations in share prices without actually owning the shares, 
**Options** - call options, put options
**Foreign Exchange**
Commodities
Copy Trading Platforms

## Careful
**Listed Commercial Property** - Large trusts that invest in diversified portfolio of commercial property listed on stock exchange (Office buildings, shopping centres, hotels etc.)
	Often called REITs or LPTS 
	Receive regular rent - so pay regular payments
	But they often have a large amount of debt
**Listed Investment Companies (LICs)** - companies that invest in other companies listed on the stock exchange, buying shares in that company gives you diversified portfolio of other companies, like a mutual fund listed on stock exchange, Different from ETFs because LICs try to pick stocks and sectors rather than track an index
