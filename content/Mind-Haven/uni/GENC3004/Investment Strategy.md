
# Borrowing to invest

## Margin loan
Different from an investment loan that has property as collateral.
1. Uses the shares as collateral on the loan
2. Offered by banks through stockbrokers (Commsec)
3. Higher interest rates than property loans
4. Subject to 'margin call' if the shares drop sharply in value
5. Flexible payments options such as 'capitalising interest' - interest just gets added to principal outstanding on the loan and isn't paid
6. Can be very easy to obtain if you have a good income

## Capitalising interest
You are charged interest on the margin loan but they just add it onto the principal outstanding of the loan so that it doesn't affect your 'net cash flow' 
if price gains > margin loan interest then LVR will fall over time

the interest is still an allowable deduction for tax purposes so investment can be negatively geared (reduces tax) but positive cash flow from the dividends (increases cash)

## LVR
$LVR = \frac{Debt}{Assets} = \frac{Margin Loan}{Market Value of Shares}$

**Portfolio has 3 different LVRs**
Current LVR - Margin loan divided by current market value shares
Base LVR - Maximum LVR based on the shares in the portfolio, this will depend on the volatility and liquidity of those shares as published in 'Approved List'
Margin Call LVR - LVR at which you will receive the dreaded 'margin call' 

### Dreaded 'Margin Call'
Exceeded the margin call LVR because you borrowed to aggressively and then
1. Sharp decline in share prices over short-period time
2. Slow decline in share prices over long-period time
3. Flat prices but capitalised interest caused LVR to increase slowly
Solution
1. Use financial slack to pay down margin loan
2. Sell shares - Usually bad if they have just fallen in value

## What is safe LVR
Many diversified ETFs have a base LVR of about 75%
	which usually means the margin call LVR = 75% +   5% = 80%
You need to be able to handle a 50% fall in market values
	most stock market crashes are about 30% - the worst are about 50% in developed markets
if prior to fall in market prices
	value of investments (assets) = 100,000
	value of margin loan (debt) = 40,000 (LVR = 40%)
If the assets fall in value by 50%
	value of investments = 50,000
	value of margin loan = 40,000 (LVR = 80%)
	only just on the threshold of margin call
	any margin call would be minor and you should be able to handle it from financial slack

Investment loans (using property) are preferred to margin loans
Avoid using Contracts for Difference (CFDs) for financial leverage
Positive to cash flow is preferred to negative gearing
Have you considered the costs of financial distress
	what if you lose your job for a significant period (in recession)
	How would you meet a significant margin call
Do you have the self control to avoid speculation
Are you investing with a very long-term time horizons 10+ years
Familiarity with tax implications

# Longevity and sequencing risk

## Longevity Risk
Risk that you will outlive your retirement savings:
1. Investing too conservatively - low returns and slow capital erosion
2. Investing too aggressively - exposed to fast fall in stock market
3. Relationship breakdown - asset redistribution and higher expenses

## Sequencing Risk
Risk that the order and timing of investments and returns is unfavourable
It is most significant during last 10 years of accumulation phase and first 10 years of retirement income phase

Need to watch out for this between ages 50 and 75

**Example**
Susan is aged 50 and has $500,000 in superannuation
She is contributing $20,000 per year

## Balancing sequencing and longevity risk

Sequencing risk - risk of large negative returns at the wrong time - lower risk
Longevity risk - risk of living longer than your savings last - higher returns

## Sequencing and Longevity Risk Strategies
1. Age pension may provide a 'cushion' as assets and income fall
2. Accept and adapt ... cut back in living expenses
3. Remember that declining health could mean more frugal lifestyle
4. May be able to obtain some financial help from family (children)
5. Use conservative assumptions when forecasting
6. Diversify and avoid speculation (especially by bored retirees)
7. Invest next year of retirement income in cash
8. Invest next 4 years of retirement income in fixed interest
9. Buy a lifetime annuity if interest rates are high (transfer risk)

# Risk profile
The higher the targeted expected return, the higher the risk, which results in higher volatility in the short-term

## Investment Risk Profile Tools
Helps you to understand how you feel about risk and how you might behave when experiencing losses
Provides guidelines on how to allocate investment funds amongst major asset categories (cash, fixed interest, shares, property)
Risk profile influenced by values, personality, experiences and education

## Risk Profile Types
1. Very conservative - averse to growth investments and prefer income investments
2. Conservative - Most income investments with small proportion in growth investments
3. Average investor - Balanced between income and growth investments
4. Growth investor - Mostly growth investments
5. Aggressive growth investor - Mostly growth investments but including some financial leverage

## Risk Profiling Tools
Various organisations provide online assessment tools
Riskprofiling.com provide a free sample for students

Risk profile is biased by heightened emotions
	Happiness makes investors more tolerant to risks
	Stress and fear makes investors less tolerant to risks - stress and fear trigger the release of cortisol that includes 'fight or flight' responses

## Strategic Asset Allocation (SAA)
Decides an appropriate long-term asset allocation
Rebalances to the original allocation periodically
	Asset allocation will deviate from target due to different returns of each category
	Sell asset categories that have outperformed
	Invest the proceeds into categories that have underperformed
Does not require ability to 'time the market'
Focuses attention on long-term investment strategy
Helps to avoid human bias to speculate on short-term 'opportunities'

## Types of Asset Allocation
Strategic Asset Allocation (SAA)
	Long-term target allocation with period rebalancing to original allocation
	Example: Cash 10%, Fixed Interest 20%, Commercial Property 20%, Shares 50%
Tactical Asset Allocation (TAA)
	Short-term deviations from SAA to profit from mis-pricing in asset categories
	Example: you think there is a stock-market bubble so - Shares - 10%, Fixed Interest +10%
Dynamic Asset Allocation (DAA)
	Continual adjustments to long-term target based on asset category performance
	Targets are in constant flux

TAA is too prone to human bias and DAA is for professional fund managers

## Things to consider

Financial literacy and experience
	More alternatives are available with higher financial education and experience

## Deciding on your strategic asset allocation
1. Cash buffer is in 'cash'
	cash buffer is minimum balance in your bank transaction account to pay bills on time
2. Financial slack is in 'cash' and/or 'financial interest'
	Financial slack is your 'save to spend' and emergency funds
3. Funds 'required' within 5 years in low-risk investments
	fixed interest (term deposits), low-risk infrastructure or listed property trusts (low leverage)
4. Determine strategic asset allocation for remaining investments
	Consider life-stage, investment objectives, time-horizon, risk-profile and diversification
5. Consider adding financial leverage when appropriate
	Consider higher risk but also higher expected long-term returns, tax advantages
	Time-horizon must be 10+ years and you must be self-controlled (human bias)

## Managing our own irrationality and bias
1. take broad view of wealth, prospects and objectives
2. Educate yourself on the reasons for long-term financial goal setting to help overcome a tendency to be attracted to short-term investments
3. Be aware of propensity for overconfidence and optimism and the negatives associated with overconfidence
4. Assess whether you have realistic view of 'the odds' with investments especially when attracted to a risk venture
5. Have a deep understanding of tolerance for risk

## FPA Portfolio Construction Process
1. Understanding the client's objectives
2. Understanding the client's risk tolerance
3. Determine the strategic asset allocation (SAA)
4. Testing the appropriateness of the SAA
5. Discuss the trade-offs, implications and alternatives
6. Determining the investment process that suits the client's needs
7. Discuss the investment structure that meets the client's specific circumstances and preferences
8. Recommending specific investments and structures
9. Ongoing reviews

# Investment tips

**Save to invest**
at least 20% of gross income and never plan to spend it - includes additional superannuation contributions
kicks of the 'investment income cycle'

**Invest in what you understand**
Sophisticated investors avoid investments they don't understand
Avoid unnecessary complexity
increase your knowledge over time
understand the likely outcomes in possible future scenarios (risks)
understand what events would trigger catastrophic loss

**Go with mainstream assets**
Understand the risks
Diversify
Diversify over time
Invest based on investment time horizon
Avoid speculation
Build your knowledge and experience
Take action

### Phase 1a: Extreme Saving
1. Obtain graduate position earning a basic full-time salary
2. radical surgery to cut living expenses so you can save 70%
3. Build minimum of $5,000 cash buffer in transaction account
4. Build minimum of $10,000 financial slack in savings
5. save an additional $10,000 and invest into ETF that tracks ASX200 - use regular stock broke for this
6. regularly invest additional savings into ETF keeping records - best to do in $5,000 increments to minimise transaction fees
Objective is to build $20,000 in ETF

### Phase 1b: Add Financial Leverage
Once you have $20,000 saved in your ETF
1. Add margin loan to your share trading account
2. borrow $10,000 using margin loan and invest in ETF (LVR 33%)
3. capitalise the interest
4. continue saving additional funds into ETF
5. With time and experience, adjust LVR up to 40%
Objective to build $100,000 in ETF
	with margin loan of $40,000 equity $60,000

### Phase 2a: Buy first property
Once you built your ETF to $100,000 ($60,000 in equity, $40,000 margin loan, 40% LVR)
1. Apply for a $500,000 home loan with parent security guarantee
2. Buy older non-trendy 2 bedroom apartment for $500,000
3. Rent out second room to a border on a 'non-commercial basis'
