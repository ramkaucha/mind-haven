
## Money and happiness

**Happiness has four components:**
1. Sustained positive emotion (positive affect)
2. Recovery from negative emotion
3. Pro-social behaviour and generosity
4. Positive mind-wandering, mindfulness and 'affective stickiness'

**Causes of happiness**
1. Inborn temperament - there is a genetic component to feeling life satisfaction and pleasure (Positive affect (PA))
2. Attitudes - Being optimistic and positive (PA), socially outgoing and generous to others
3. Activities - Actively engaged in meaningful work (or study), exercise and social engagement.
4. Demographics - income, wealth, living situation, rule of law and political freedom

### Utility theory
More money allows you to satisfy more needs (utility), but each dollar gives less happiness than last one

## Money and human bias

**Humans are predictably irrational**
The brain uses heuristics when making decisions - it takes short-cuts due to limited time, information, ability, mental energy and attention
These short-cuts often result in sub-optimal or irrational decisions
Irrational decisions can be predictable in certain situations

### Problem with classical utility theory
it assumes happiness is purely rational based on objective states
it assumes we don't compare with social peers (relative income)
it assumes we don't compare with past (gains or losses)
it assumes we don't compare actual versus expected (gains or losses)

we react more to perceived gains and losses against a 'status quo'
we over-react to perceived losses
we especially over-react to the prospect of a perceived future loss

**Prospect theory and happiness**
1. more is not necessarily better
2. what matters is gains and losses against status quo
3. we choose status quo and this 'frames' comparisons
4. split up good experiences will normally yield more happiness
5. combine bad experience will normally minimise unhappiness
6. Avoid unfavourable comparisons
7. Be aware of irrationality with the prospect of future losses

Adaptation spoils happiness
adaptability is the key to success
makes happiness illusive
usually we adapt to good things in our lives, and they become part of the status quo (home, food, people, devices...)
then they no longer trigger gains and losses - this is called *Hedonic Treadmill*

**What influences the rate of adaptation**
Novelty - we have not experience the event before
Surprise - we didn't expect the event to happen the way it did
Uncertainty - we are not sure how to interpret the event
Variability - the experience keeps changing over time

**Human bias and money happiness**
Loss aversion - are we acting irrationally due to losses incurred or feared
Status-Quo bias - do we fail to take action and make changes
Overconfidence effect - do we overly attribute past successes to skill
affect bias - have we 'fallen in love' with one option
Saliency bias - have we been influenced by stories or analogies
Confirmation bias - have credible alternatives really been considered
Mental Accounting bias - have we created arbitrary 'buckets' for expenses
Availability Bias - would different decision be made in a year
Anchoring Bias - where do the numbers come from
Halo Effect - are we influenced by the attractiveness of the messenger
Sunk Cost Bias and Endowment Effect - Are people biased by past decisions

### Money personality profiles
1. Achieving money worshipers - worship money as their success and budget money carefully
2. careless money admirers - value financial success but do not budget money carefully
3. apathetic money managers - money is not evil not a motivator and have high intrinsic job and life satisfaction
4. money repellent individual - consider money to be evil and is not a measure of access

## Money happiness principles
**Define your own values and goals** - Focus on achieving intrinsic goals consistent with your own values rather than extrinsic approval . From others or financial success especially for 'achieving money worshipers'. Tracking and measuring success adds to feeling of 'achievement'
**Avoid replacing too much emphasis on 'things'** - placing too high importance on accumulating income and wealth as a source of happiness (materialism) can be detrimental. We can adapt and can end up on a hedonic treadmill
**Invest in relationships** - most happiness is determined by quality and breadth of relationships. Each interaction is unique - making it difficult to adapt for higher cognition - triggering gains and losses. They are also an important component of pro-social behaviour
**Invest in experiences** - experiences require higher cognition and are more likely to trigger gains and losses. Young people gain more happiness from extraordinary experiences. Older people gain more happiness from ordinary experiences. Experiences with other people have the most significant impact
**Help others rather than yourself** - Being generous with time and gifts with others. Provides more happiness than most people realise
**Focus more on time than money** - Focus more on spending time on happiness rather than spending money on happiness. Start making a list of things that bring you the most joy in life
**Maintain some financial slack** - Financial slack (emergency savings) provides 'peace of mind' when experiencing an unexpected shock or crises. Keep emergency fund with at least 6+ months of living expenses
**Actively work to serve others** - Active employment (paid/unpaid). Builds relationships with others creates a feeling of outward-focussed purpose
**Buy regular small pleasures over big ones** - Prospect theory predicts that separating gains will elicit a greater perceived gain. Although for young people, this needs to be balanced with extraordinary experiences being preferred over ordinary ones
**Enjoy 'free' anticipation** - the person who buys a good gets X units of pleasure, while the person who anticipates then buys it gets X + Y units of pleasure. Delayed gratification is highly correlated with health, wealth & happiness
**Happiness is in the details** - day to day life experience have biggest impact on happiness. Avoid seeking happiness in abstract long-term plans as they tend to be light on detail
**Practice contentment and thankfulness** - 'counting blessings' slows down the rate of adaptation while also eliciting a perceived gain. 'Minimal social interactions' of greetings, thanking and conversing. Consider starting a 'gratitude journal'.
**Avoid unfavourable comparisons** - Prospect theory predicts that choosing an unfavourable 'status quo' will trigger off an unnecessary and adverse sense of loss.
**Looking after health** - there is a clear connection between mind and body. make sure to get enough sleep, nutrition and physical activity
**If you pursue happiness you won't find it** - hedonism is a hedonic treadmill.

## Purpose, value and principles

**Values**
Quality or standard of behaviour that tell us what is 'good'
Expressed in general terms and focusses on what we value. Often expressed in one or two words, such as 'thriftiness' or 'honesty' 
**Principles**
A specific rule governing behaviour that tell us what is 'right'
Specific and applied statements outlining how we may (or may not) achieve our values. Often expressed in statements as "I'll never ..." or "I'll always"
**Purpose**
Provides our reason for being
It explains why our values and principles are important
### Example of values and principles

| Value                | Principle                                                                                                      |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Thriftiness          | I'll always practice contentment and avoid buying things just because they are 'new' or to impress others      |
| Open-mindedness      | I'll practice listening, compassion and empathy so that I can understand other people's thinking and behaviour |
| Fairness             | I'll never treat people unfairly by making judgments based on their gender, sexual orientation, age ...        |
| Honesty              | Never lie, prevent harm, hurting feelings                                                                      |
| Relationships matter | I'll never be unfaithful to my friends or partner.                                                             |

**Why do values matter**
1. Conducive for happiness via intrinsic motivation
2. Important for determining ethical frameworks
3. Helpful for making ethical decisions involving trade-offs
4. Influence pro-social behaviour and generosity
5. Help you set your long-term life and financial goals
6. Assist you to choose a career and develop a career strategy
7. Influence strategies, tactics and product selection to achieve goals

==For  the financial plan - choose between 3 and 10 values==

### Identifying your core values
1. existential or spiritual beliefs
2. Cultural and family background
3. Think of 3 to 6 people that you admire or love. Why?
4. Look back - what were the most bitter or sweet moments ? Why ?
5. Look forward - what will people say at your funeral ? Why ?
6. Start narrow: Short Schawartz Value Survey (10 values)
7. Go wide: Values Checklist by Russ Harris (50 + values)
8. Free online inventories - Life values inventory by Crace and Brown
9. Observe yourself, talk to trusted friends and family and learn.

**Values can change over time**
Changes in values tend not to be 'chaotic' - but the composition of your top 5 - 7 and their importance do change
Life-changing events (rather than age) causes these changes
Increase in importance of any one value is associated with an increase in importance of compatible values, a decrease in importance of conflicting values

**Life principles** - Life principles explain 'how' you plan to live-out your values
**Life purpose** - an overall statement of the 'why' behind your values and principles. You can have different 'purpose' statements for work and personal life.

## Nominal and Real Rates of Return

### Price inflation
the price of goods and services usually increases over time because growth in aggregate demand exceeds growth in aggregate supply
this is called 'price inflation' and affects the cost of living
measure by the Consumer Price Index (CPI) - measured by the Australian Bureau of Statistics

### Interest rates and inflation
RBA influences interest rates
They set overnight 'cash rate' for borrowing and lending between banks
All interest rates are based on cash rate + risk premium
The main objective of the RBA is stable prices
The target rate of inflation is 2% to 3% per annum

==Course long-term inflation = 2.5% p.a ==

### Fisher effect from economics
describes the relationship between inflation, real and nominal rates of return
nominal rate of return* ~~ Real rate of return + inflation rate
nominal rate of return = actual rate of return earned by an investment
real rate of return = actual rate of return controlled for changes in the cost of living (inflation)
e.g. inflation = 2%, real return = 2%, then nominal return = 4%

real returns over the long run tend to be independent of inflation

## Savings Calculation
###  Short to medium term (0 - 5 years)
financial buffer, financial slack,  moving out of home, motor vehicle, overseas holiday, deposit on property.
Best to use nominal returns (including inflation)

### Long term (5 + years)
debt reduction on property, financial independence (super), children's school fees
Best to use real returns (excluding inflation)

### Compound interest = future value
Simple interest - interest is calculated on principal amount saved only
Compound interest - interest is calculated based on both principal and past interest

### Future value of single cash flow
$F_n = P \times (1 + r)^n$
P = cash flow that is being moved
n = number of periods it is being moved
r = effective rate of return per period
F = value of the cash flow 'P' after it has been moved 'n' periods right
### Present value of single cash flow
$P = \frac{F_n}{(1+r)^n}$
F = cash flow that is being moved
n = number of periods it is being moved
r = effective rate of return per period
F = value of the cash flow 'P' after it has been moved 'n' periods left

### Annuity
Set of regular cash flows.
For an 'ordinary' annuity, the first cash flow occurs at the end of the first period and the cash flows are always level (not growing)
Useful for projects with level cash flows, loans and financial products

**Future value of annuity**
Calculating the value of the cash flows (with first cash flow at end of first period) at the **END** of the regular series of payments
here 

$F_n = C \times [\frac{(1+r)^n - 1}{r}]$
C = regular cash flows that are being moved to the right
n = number of cash flows
r = effective rate of return per period
F = value of regular series of 'n' cash flows of 'C' at the end (right)
