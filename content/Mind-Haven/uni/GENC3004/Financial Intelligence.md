The science and art of turning financial information into 'meaningful story' to help people make better decisions

Involves understanding the past, current and the future, understanding the 'big picture' and 'the detail', communication and decisions

Financial intelligence involves:
1. Understanding current situations
2. Set goals for future life stages that are consistent with your values
3. Identify the gaps between your current situation and future goals
4. Identify strategies to close the gaps
5. Identify and manage risks
6. Implement your Strategies

## Controlling expenses:
### Preparing a budget
Estimate your income - how much is coming in?
List your expenditure - how much is going out?
Surplus/Deficit

**Cash flow budget** - estimate of future..
	cash inflows (income)
	cash outflows (expenses, purchases, loan repayments)
	usually on a monthly and annual basis with a commitment to make improvements

Key thing is whether the regular cash coming in (income) is enough to cover the cash going out (expenses and loan payments)

#### Reasons to prepare budget
1. process can give ideas about how to improve outcomes
2. Identifies future expenses that require a savings plan
3. Allows you to compare budget with actual numbers (variance analysis)
4. Identifies areas of over-spending and/or under-spending
5. Helps us to be accountable and stayed disciplined
6. Helps us to save more
7. Improves financial intelligence and skills

**Budgets are useful when**
1. moving out of home
2. moving to new country
3. getting married
4. buying your first home
5. school
6. approaching retirement

[Budget Planner](https://moneysmart.gov.au/budgeting/budget-planner)

#### Middle of the road estimate
Estimates should be in the middle of what is expected to happen
Forecasts get more accurate with experience and stability
![[Pasted image 20241231151329.png]]

**A budget involves ... learning from the past**
1. learn from the past
2. identify and remove extraordinary items
3. calculate average monthly spend per category
4. make assumptions about interest rates and investment returns
5. identify how your work or living situation may change next year
6. estimate impact of these changes on your future income and expenses
7. add a 'buffer' for extraordinary or unexpected items - at least 10% of regular expenses
Making improvements
8. identify ways to increase income - work longer hours, career advancement, raise, bonuses, investment income
9. identify ways to reduce expenses - cut subscriptions, use public transport, cover lunch, dine out less, etc...
10. identify ways to reduce risk to income and expenses - looking after physical and mental health, learn new skills, buy insurance, maintain car
12. Track actual income and expenses and compare with budget (variance analysis) - track actuals against budget, self-correct behavior, learn from the process

#### Tips of budget preparation
1. track expenses for a few months first
2. include your partner in the process
3. be realistic ... don't set goals that you will definitely fail
4. include a generous buffer to allow for errors or unexpected expenses
5. avoid being harsh by including `fun` in your budget
6. avoid being stingy by including `gifts` in your budget
7. aim to save at least 20% of your income (profit margin)
### Tracking Expenses
Tracking income and expenses.

**Alternatives to tracking expenses**
Manual tracking - receipts from shops, note down income and expenses each day, add them up end of the month
Download transactions - buying everything in debit card, downloading transactions at the end of each month from online banking and adding up the income and expenditure
automated tracking - some banks provide automated tracking tools (Ubank, Up Bank), and some banks can send data automatically to third-party services (Frollo)

### Bank Accounts
Having a system of bank accounts to help us save and invest

#### Bank Transaction Accounts
Mainly used to help buy things and pay bills
DC used for electronic transactions
Monthly account fees

#### Bank Savings Account
Mainly for savings that may be needed within 12 months
Often have bonus interest rates if linked to Transaction Account
Instant transfer of funds to other accounts
Usually cannot use to directly pay bills or buy things
Usually no monthly account fees
Government guarantees on balances up to $250,000


Transaction account balance should never get close to zero (LOL)
There should be a upper limit, a return point (cash buffer) and a lower limit (that's when you want to top up from a savings account)

#### Emergency Fund
Minimum of $2,000, and then build up over time to 6 months of living expenses

Provides peace of mind for paying rent and mortgage payments if you suddenly lose your job, have a health problem or there is a crisis
Another name for this is 'Financial Slack' since it provides you with the 'flexibility' to handle a downturn in your income

#### Three Account System
1. Regular payments account
	Receives your salary or wage from employer
	Automatically pays regular bills (rent, phone, electricity etc) and periodic payments
	sweeps money into weekly spending account and savings account
2. Weekly spending account
	Receives a fixed amount each week from Regular Payments Account
	Use to buy things either cash withdrawals or a linked Debit Card
3. Savings Account(s)
	Receives a fixed amount each week from Regular Payments Account
	Used for medium to long-term savings
	Multiple accounts savings accounts can be used for different savings goals

## Good and Bad Debt
Debt is a short-term or long-term loan from a bank or some other source
#### Good Debt:
When it is used to buy a quality home in a good location - long-term control over living expenses assuming that the value of the home increases over the long-term
When it is used for investing in shares or property over the long-term - assuming that the return on the investment exceeds the interest rate over the long-term
When it is necessary to generate future income - student loans (HECS) may be needed to fund a degree, car loan to buy a car.
![[Pasted image 20241231161927.png]]
Getting loan for investment.
Only borrow money to buy things that are expected to increase in value

#### Bad Debt
When it is used to fund expenses or pay bills. Buy asserts that are expected to decrease in value (especially cars) or make up for a lack of savings
These are things such as credit card, buy now pay later, car loans (unless absolutely necessary), borrowing money.

**Reasons why people get into credit card debt**
1. Impulsive behaviour
2. Bad habits
3. High fixed and variable expenses relative to income
4. Poor planning (no budget)
5. lack of personal discipline to stick to budget
6. addicted to loyalty point schemas
7. bad luck

**Steps to get out of CC debt**
1. Acknowledge that you have a problem
2. Seek help from debt helpline if needed ndh.org.au
3. talk to bank about consolidating debt - stop any new transactions, consolidate credit card debt into personal loan with fixed repayments
4. Track expenses
5. Saving at least 10% of income to pay down bad debt


## Three Financial Statements
All of the financial statements are linked
they are important for households, banks, organisations, and government
understanding them is an element of financial intelligence 
1. Balance Sheet - Tells you your wealth at a point in time - important for tracking long-term wealth creation
2. Income Statement - Tells you why your wealth changes over a period of time - Important for short-term control of expenses (and understanding why your wealth is changing)
3. Cash Flow Statement - Tells you how your cash changed over a period of time - Important for obtaining loans and making sure you can pay your bills on time
![[Pasted image 20241231163611.png]]
### Balance Sheet
Tells you your wealth at a point in time
important for tracking long-term wealth creation

Balance sheet: Assets - Liabilities = Wealth
Wealth is also called 'Net Worth', 'Net Assets' or simply 'Equity'
#### Asset Examples
Something you own that provides a future benefit
- transaction account (debit card)
- savings account - short-term low risk savings
- share investment - long-term savings (high return with uncertainty)
- superannuation - long-term financial independence (retirement savings)
- vehicle - used to get you to and from public transport, work or study
- home contents - furniture, clothes, computers
- property - used to protect your cost of living in the long-run and create wealth
- university degree
#### Liability Example:
Something owed to others that must be repaid in the future
- buy now pay later - allowing you to buy now and pay in instalments
- credit card - allowing buy now and pay end of month
- student loan - allowing you to do your degree and pay later
- personal loan - buy now pay it later
- vehicle loan - allowing you to buy a vehicle now and pay later
- property loan - allowing you to buy a property now and pay it later
- investment loan - allowing you to buy investment now and pay for them later

Balance sheet - measured at point in time, round to nearest dollar
best treat all your course fees as an assert (Uni degree) and any student loans as a liability (HECS)

#### Comparisons
Balance sheets can be compared against another point in time
change in wealth will be our profit over this period

profit may include non-cash income, and expenses such as decrease in value of vehicle (depreciation expense) and gains in the market value of investments (at fair value)
![[Pasted image 20241231165928.png]]

**Fair value of historic cost**
With personal balance sheet you have a choice
1. Record value of asset at original cost (historic cost account) - usually less accumulated depreciation if it is a motor vehicle or other physical asset. When you record it at original cost less total depreciation it is called 'book value'
2. Record value of asset at current market value (fair value account) - this is a rough estimate of how much you could sell the asset for today
**Property valuation**
Record the value of a property at 'historic cost' rather than 'market value'
this is the original purchase price + any significant improvements (renovations)
this means that change in wealth on your balance sheet is only from earning income, keeping expenses under control and investment returns - rather than 'lazy' property price increases or short-term property price decrease

**Vehicle depreciation**
Halves in value every 3.5 years
the amount it decreases in value each period is called 'depreciation'
depreciation is a non-cash expense from owning a vehicle, is an expensive 'invisible' expense that most people miss

#### Depreciation
vehicles, computers and many other things decrease in value over time
decrease in value is an expense called 'depreciation'
it is not a cash flow
two methods:
1. diminishing value method (curve)
	decreases by fixed percentage each year based on value at end of last period
2. Prime cost method (straight-line)
	decreases by fixed dollar amount each year

#### Share investment changes
Share investment increase/decrease in value at any period
1. record the value at current market value (fair value accounting)
	your balance sheet will reflect the current value of your investment
	changes in wealth (and implied profit) will have greater volatility
2. Record the value at original cost (historic cost accounting)
	balance sheet will reflect the original funds that you invested
	Changes in wealth (and implied profit) will be less volatile

### Income Statement
Tells you why your wealth changes over a period of time
Important for short-term control of expenses (and understanding why your wealth is changing)

Income statement: Income - Expenses = Profit

Balance sheet captures the flow of income and expenses
Wealth is the cumulative sum of all past profits

**Income examples**
Benefits received from work, investment, family or others - that do not need to be repaid
Personal income - wages and salaries associated from your work (human capital)
Investment income - interest, rent, dividends or distributions from your investments (financial capital)
Investment gains - profit from selling investments or assets at a gain. Losses from selling investments can be treated as a 'negative' investment gain
Financial support that does not need to be repaid - family support, scholarships, social security

**Cash flows that aren't income**
Selling physical assets (car, clothes, furniture) 
	if the amount is less than its value on your balance sheet, the difference is an expense
	if the amount is more than its value on your balance sheet, the difference is income

borrowing money (student loans, car loans, credit cards, home loans)
	the amount received is a cash flow (and is not income)
	if the money doesn't need to be repaid (family loan), it is a 'gift' which is a form of income

**Expense examples**
costs incurred as your work, study, invest and live your life
housing and utilities - rent, interest on home loan, electricity, water, rates, internet, maintenance
food and drink - fresh food and groceries, takeaway, coffee or tea, alcohol...
work and study - student fees, textbooks, computers, mobile phone
transport - train and buses , rideshares, car
financial and insurance - fees, interest on loans, life insurance
leisure - dining out, gifts, entertainment, holidays, subscriptions, hobbies and interest
health - medical expenses, health insurance, fitness
clothing - work, leisure, sport..

**Cash flow that isn't an expense**
University course fees
repayment of the principal of a loan
	loan payments are normally part principal repayment and part interest expense
	entire loan payment each period is a cash outflow
	interest component is an expense and a cash outflow
	principal repayment is a cash outflow but it is not an expense
credit card or buy-now-pay-later payments

#### Categories
there is no 'standard' categories for income and expenses (30 is too many, 3-4 too less) - choose what works for you

#### Time period
income statement is always measured over a period of time
'Month' is a good balance for most people
	frequent enough that you can make corrections to spending behaviour
	long enough to 'average out' high expenditure in any individual day or week
	'last 12 months' can be good to track as well
Measured over last month. Year to date gives 'big picture' - assuming situation hasn't changed

compare these against periods such as last 2 months, percentages, over the year etc..

#### Large expenses
Useful to 'spread' large 'lumpy' expenses over several periods
e.g.
$360 annual insurance - $30 per month for 12 months
$2,400 laptop computer - $50 per month for 4 years
$120 clothing - $40 per month for 3 months
### Cash Flow Statement
Tells you how your cash changed over a period of time
Important for obtaining loans and making sure you can pay your bills on time

Cash flow statement: Cash in - Cash Out = Cash Flow

**Cash flow is different from income and expenses**
Borrowing money is a cash inflow but not income
Paying off the principal of loan is a cash outflow but not an expense
buying a vehicle is a cash outflow but not an expense
decrease in value of a vehicle is an expense but not a cash flow
selling a vehicle is a cash inflow but not income

but earning wage/salary is income and a cash inflow 
buying most small things is normally an expense and a cash outflow
#### Loans
Banks focus on cash flow when applying for loans, they are usually interested in regular and reliable cash inflows (income) and cash outflows (expenses and loan repayments)

Negative cash flow means your cash will gradually reduce to zero
Positive cash flow means your cash will gradually increase
#### Cash inflows
After-tax salary/wages, financial support received from scholarship, parents etc., interest on savings account, dividends or distribution on shares, rent from an investment property, funds borrowed (liability), proceeds from sale of assets or investments

#### Cash Outflow
Living expenses, purchase a new assets (vehicle, computer), purchase of a new investment (shares/property), credit card payments, home loan payments, other loan payments

Non-cash items are not to be included (depreciation expense, unrealised gains)

Cash Flow statement is a record of the past
Cash flow budget is about the future based on your expected future situation, usually with a commitment to make improvements

#### Operating Leverage
If most expenses are fixed, you have 'high operating leverage', a short-term fall in income can result in a large loss - but short-term increase in income can result in the large profit

if most expenses are variable, you have 'low operating leverage', a short-term fall in income can result in a smaller loss - but a short-term increase in income only results in small profit

![[Pasted image 20241231183906.png]]

#### Operating Leverage changes with Life Stage

| Life Stage                                | Operating Leverage | Risk of Loss |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------ |
| Living at home with parents               | Low                | Low          |
| Single living out of income               | Moderate           | Moderate     |
| Couple with dependent young children      | High               | High         |
| Couple with independent adult childern    | Moderate           | Moderate     |
| Financially dependent retiree (poor)      | High               | High         |
| Financially independent retiree (wealthy) | Low                | Low          |


1. Fixed and variable costs will change with your situation and life stages
	Single student, single worker, stable couple, couple with children, retired couple
2. Income uncertainty varies significantly between different people
	Casual work versus full-time contract
	Seniority, experience and individual performance
	Company, industry and economic volatility
3. Support from extended family can make a difference
	Assistance with the first home and ability to 'move back home' in worst-case scenario
4. Flexibility and contentment can make a difference
	Flexibility to significantly cut costs if income decreases
	Contentment with existing spending levels if income increases
